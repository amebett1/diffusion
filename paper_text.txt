Abstract
Automated visual inspection (AVI) systems increasingly rely on deep learning-based object detection to deliver fast and
consistent surface defect identiﬁcation. However, the scarcity of defect image data and the shift in visual feature distributions
caused by product variations remain signiﬁcant challenges that hinder the practical deployment of deep learning systems.
To address these challenges, we propose a synthetic defect generation pipeline based on an inpainting diffusion model. We
introduce a Context-Aware Mask Generation (CAMG) module to ensure conforming mask generation required for realistic
defect image synthesis. The proposed CAMG module employs a Self-DIstillation with NO labels (DINO) vision foundation
model to automatically learn defect placement rules on product images. By synthesizing defects onto the non-defective
images of new products via an inpainting approach, our method effectively bridges the distributional gap caused by product
variations, enhancing robustness in New Product Introduction (NPI) scenarios. We validate the effectiveness of our method
on footwear and printed circuit board (PCB) datasets. The experimental results demonstrate that incorporating the synthetic
defect images consistently improves the mean average precision (mAP), precision, and recall performances of various defect
detection models, with the most notable gains observed in the challenging NPI scenario.

Related Work
Deep Learning for Industrial Defect Detection
Deep learning-based object detectors, particularly single-
stage methods based on You Only Look Once (YOLO) (Red-
mon et al., 2016) and Detection Transformer (DETR) (Zhao
et al., 2024) architectures, have been widely adopted for
AVI systems. Their end-to-end architecture is suitable for
real-time inference and multi-class defect detection, which
is a critical requirement for in-line inspection in manufactur-
ing. However, supervised object detectors rely on large-scale
labeled datasets. Insufﬁcient training data and distribution
shifts can result in a severe overﬁtting problem. Data aug-
mentation (Wang et al., 2025) and domain adaptation (Wang
et al., 2023) have been widely studied to overcome these chal-
lenges. Although domain adaptation methods such as domain
adversariallearning(Ganinetal.,2016;Lietal.,2023)enable
end-to-end feature alignment between distinct input distri-
butions, they require representative target domain samples
during training, which is unavailable when no defect images
of the target product exist prior to production. Moreover,
these methods often focus on global feature alignment and
may inadvertently suppress the ﬁne-grained local features
essential for precise defect localization. Consequently, recent
paradigms have shifted towards a generative data augmen-
tation approach leveraging large-scale foundation models to
enrich the existing dataset.
Synthetic Defect Image Generation
Data augmentation is a widely adopted strategy to address
data scarcity in industrial defect detection. Early approaches
utilized copy-and-paste methods (Li et al., 2021) and GAN-
based methods (Zhang et al., 2021) to synthesize defect
images. However, they often fail to generate realistic textures
or seamless blends with the background. Recently, latent
diffusion models (Rombach et al., 2022) have emerged as
a powerful alternative, outperforming previous methods in
generating high-ﬁdelity images. In particular, an inpainting
diffusion model, a speciﬁc type of diffusion model that is
trained to ﬁll the mask area seamlessly onto a given input
image, showed strong capability to generate local defect

Mask Generation for Inpainting Diffusion
In the application of inpainting diffusion models for defect
image generation, mask generation serves as a critical step
that determines the spatial location and the speciﬁc category
of the defect to be synthesized. In various industrial domains,
defect masks must be carefully located for defect image gen-
eration. For instance, in our case study of footwear product
defect detection, a ’Loose Thread End’ defect must be placed
on semantically valid regions such as the stitching area, rather
than on incompatible materials like the rubber sole. However,
approaches for mask generation in previous studies show
several limitations. Reusing defect masks from the training
set or using custom-drawn masks can ensure mask valid-
ity, but hinder the full automation of the data generation
pipeline (Chen et al., 2025; Gui et al., 2025; Song et al.,
2025). Foreground sampling (Choi et al., 2025; Rother et al.,
2004) distinguishes products from the background, but lacks
granularity for precise defect placement within product parts.
Finally, while integrating mask generation directly into the
generative model can be an effective strategy, such an end-to-
end approach presents low interpretability (Hu et al., 2024).
To address these problems, we introduce a learnable Context-
Aware Mask Generation (CAMG) module that explicitly
models the joint distribution of defect location, type, and size
conditioned on the input image. Based on vision foundation
model features, CAMG enables semantically consistent and
contextually valid defect synthesis, particularly for industrial
products with complex surface structures.
Parameter-Efficient Fine-tuning for Diffusion Models
While large-scale diffusion models possess strong generative
capabilities, they are pre-trained on general-domain datasets
and often lack the domain-speciﬁc knowledge required to
represent industrial defects and complex product textures.
Therefore, ﬁne-tuning is essential to bridge this gap and
enable the model to capture the distinct visual characteristics
of the target products.
To achieve this while preserving the pre-trained capa-
bilities, previous studies have explored various parameter-
efﬁcient ﬁne-tuning strategies. In particular, Low-Rank
adaptation (LoRA) (Hu et al., 2022) enables layer-wise
ﬁne-tuning with a minimal number of learnable parameters.
Textual Inversion (Gal et al., 2023) and Dreambooth (Ruiz et
al., 2022) introduce learnable conditioning tokens to capture
speciﬁc concepts. These methods enable ﬁne-tuning of large-
scale generative models without the risk of overﬁtting even
with a few-shot supervision. Drawing inspiration from these
studies, our pipeline incorporates LoRA and textual inver-
sion to adapt the inpainting diffusion model to the industrial
domain.

Fine-Tuning Inpainting Diffusion Model
Inpainting Diffusion Process
We adopt the Latent Diffusion Model (LDM) (Rombach
et al., 2022) as our generative backbone. Speciﬁcally, we
employ an inpainting diffusion model that synthesizes only
masked regions based on the given image. Given an input
image I, a binary defect mask B and a defect prompt P, the
input image is ﬁrst encoded into a latent representation using
a variational autoencoder (VAE). During the denoising pro-
cess, the U-Net ϵθ predicts the noise component ϵ from the
noisy latent xt at each denoising timestamp t. In addition,
after each denoising process, the latent representation corre-
sponding to the unmasked region (1 −B) is overwritten to
preserve the background area.

Synthetic Defect Image Generation
With the given product image and defect masks, the inpaint-
ingdiffusionmodelgeneratessyntheticdefectimagesthrough
an iterative denoising process. In addition, diffusion mod-
els are known to occasionally produce undesirable outputs
because of the nature of their probabilistic generation pro-
cess (Choi et al., 2025; Garcia-Perez et al., 2025). The types
of undesirable outputs can be divided into three cases: Nor-
mal reconstruction, unrealistic defect, and non-conforming
mask generation. While the non-conforming mask gener-
ation is minimized by the CAMG module, the other two
cases can misguide the defect detector. Therefore, we intro-
duce a conﬁdence-based ﬁltering method to achieve a fully
automated generation pipeline. We employ a defect detec-
tor trained on real images to evaluate the generated images.
Then, we rank the synthetic defect instances based on their
detection conﬁdence scores and ﬁlter out the bottom k%.

Generation Process
Weﬁne-tunedapre-trainedinpaintingdiffusionmodel(Rom-

the generation stage, we randomly selected one defect patch
for each defect type using top-k sampling. Defect images
were generated by iterating 50 denoising steps. A negative
prompt ’blurry, low quality, deformed, ugly, watermark, sig-

from generating unwanted artifacts. The generation process
took 4 seconds per image under the given setting. A total of
1,931 and 829 defect images were generated in the footwear
and PCB dataset respectively.
Generation Quality Evaluation
We employ four quality metrics for the evaluation of
generated images, Foreground Structural Similarity Index
Measure (Foreground SSIM), Fréchet Inception Distance
(FID) (Heusel et al., 2018), Learned Perceptual Image
Patch Similarity (LPIPS) (Zhang et al., 2018) and pseudo
recall. Foreground SSIM computes the structural similar-
ity between the original and generated images within the
mask region, directly assessing how seamlessly the gen-
erated defect blends with its background. A higher SSIM
value indicates that the generative model successfully pre-
serves and reﬂects the structural context. FID and LPIPS are
utilized to evaluate the visual ﬁdelity and diversity of the
generated samples at the distribution level. The FID score is
low when the distribution of the synthetic images is closer
to that of the real defect images. Conversely, LPIPS mea-
sures the perceptual variance of the generated images. While
a higher LPIPS score is conventionally favored in general
image synthesis to demonstrate high diversity, an excessively

Once (YOLO) network architectures (Redmon et al., 2016),
YOLOv8, YOLOv9, and YOLO11 (Jocher & Qiu, 2024;
Jocher et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024), and two Detection
Transformer (DETR) architectures (Zhao et al., 2024) with
Vision Transformer (ViT) backbone and Resnet50 backbone
were tested in the experiment. These object detection models
are widely employed for industrial defect detection (Hus-
sain, 2023), showing cutting-edge performance at real-time
inference speeds. Experiments were conducted under two
manufacturing scenarios: The In-Distribution (ID) case and
the New Product Introduction (NPI) case. Fig. 8 conceptu-
ally illustrates the data split and generation ﬂow for both the
ID and NPI scenarios. To construct the two scenarios from
a shared dataset, a subset of products was ﬁrst randomly
designated as unseen products and withheld from the train-
ing by a product-wise split. The remaining products formed
the common training set used in both scenarios. In the ID
case, the unseen products are excluded, and we used 85% of
randomly selected retained images for training and the other
15% for validation. In the NPI case, the training set is equiva-
lent, while the validation set is replaced with images from the
withheld unseen products. Speciﬁcally, 6 out of 8 products
for footwear and 9 out of 12 products for PCB were used for
training, and the remaining products were used for validation.
Note that a small set of normal images for unseen prod-
ucts is assumed to be accessible in advance, which reﬂects
a practical NPI scenario where sample product images can
be collected before defects are observed. An IoU threshold
of 0.25 and a conﬁdence threshold of 0.25 were applied for
precision and recall measure.
All object detectors were trained under same setting
for fair comparison. The scale parameter is ’small’ for
YOLO-based models, considering the number of patch-wise
inferences required in the deployment stage (Akyon et al.,
2022; Cho et al., 2026). Models were trained for 100 epochs
using an AdamW optimizer with 1e-4 learning rate, and
with batch size 8. In addition, HSV augmentations, random
ﬂip, random rotation were applied on the training images
for better generalization. Mean average precision (mAP50),
precision, and recall metrics presented on this section are
averaged across all classes. Additional class-wise experi-
mental results are reported in Appendix Appendix A. All
performance gains are expressed in percentage points (pp).

NPI Case
In the NPI case setup, the defect detection models were
trained with additional synthetic defect images that are made
using the non-defective product images of unseen products.
Experimental results are reported in Table 6.
As presented in Table 6, the baseline models suffer from
severe performance degradation in the NPI scenario due to
the distribution shift between existing and unseen products
compared to the performance metrics in Table 3. The results
show that incorporating the synthetic defect images signif-
icantly mitigates this performance lag across all models,
resulting in higher mAP score by up to 5.7 percentage points
in the footwear dataset and 20.1 percentage points in the PCB
dataset. Improvements in precision and recall scores were up
to 12.9 pp, 18.3 pp in the footwear dataset, and 13.7 pp, 21.5
pp in the PCB dataset. The overall performance gain was
higher in the PCB dataset. This difference comes from dif-
ferent structural characteristics of the dataset. PCB images
feature more rigid structure with consistent spatial align-
ment,whichallowsmoreeffectivefeaturespaceinterpolation
between different products. Lightweight models trained with
synthetic data achieved comparable or superior performance
to the computationally expensive models trained solely on
real images. This indicates that a data-centric approach is
particularly effective than a model-centric approach in NPI
scenarios.
Discussion
Inthissection,weprovideaqualitativeanalysisandafeature-
level visualization to explore how the proposed synthetic data
generation enhances defect detection performance. Fig. 9
shows different inference results of YOLO11 models trained
with and without synthetic defect images. The model trained
without synthetic defect image does not capture the defects
on unseen products. However, the model trained with syn-
thetic data successfully captures the defect and correctly
classiﬁes the defect types.
Fig. 10 reveals how the synthetic data contributes to the
signiﬁcant performance improvements in the NPI case. The
ﬁgure illustrates the feature distributions of footwear defect
images extracted from the backbone of the YOLO11s model
trained on real images. Low-level features in (a) and deep fea-
tures in (b) correspond to the outputs of the ﬁrst and the last
convolutional blocks, respectively. In the low-level feature
space, a distinct separation between the train and valida-
tion image clusters reﬂects the visual appearance differences
between products. This discrepancy indicates a distribution
shift, which is a primary factor contributing to the perfor-
mance degradation. Conversely, in the deep feature space,
the training and validation image features are mixed. This
overlap suggests that the intrinsic characteristics required
for defect detection remain consistent. The synthetic defect
images span across both the training and validation clusters
in the low-level space, indicating that the generated images
capture the visual traits of the new product. In addition,

Conclusion
In this paper, we addressed the challenge of defect data
scarcity in dynamic manufacturing environments by propos-
ing a fully automated synthetic defect image generation
pipeline.Theproposedmethodcanbeimmediatelyappliedto
newly introduced products using only normal images, mak-
ing it particularly suited for manufacturing settings where
real defect images are rare and product designs change
frequently. Experiments across multiple detector architec-
tures on two real-world industrial datasets conﬁrmed that the
synthetic images consistently improve defect detection per-
formance,withthemostsubstantialgainsobservedintheNPI
scenario. These results suggest that automating the defect
data generation process is a practical and scalable direction
for maintaining robust inspection systems under distribution
shift.
The limitations of our study and future research oppor-
tunities are as follows. First, the current method is built on
an assumption that the defect types are ﬁxed. The generation
capability of entirely novel defect types is not studied yet,
and future work could explore few-shot or zero-shot learn-
ing techniques for CAMG and inpainting diffusion. Second,
the current study generates synthetic defect images by deter-
mining the defect’s type, size, and location. This approach
does not reﬂect other contexts, such as the defect’s orienta-
tion or severity. Enhancing the controllability of the defect
image generation model could further increase the diversity
of the dataset and improve its utility in downstream tasks.
